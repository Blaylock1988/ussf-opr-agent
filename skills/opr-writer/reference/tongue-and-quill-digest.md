# Tongue and Quill digest (AFH 33-337, 27 May 2015, Change 1): OPR-relevant rules only

The skill bundles this digest instead of the full handbook, to keep token costs low. It covers only what applies to one-line OPR bullets: editing (ch 8), bullets (ch 19), punctuation (ch 25), abbreviations (ch 26), capitalization (ch 27), numbers (ch 28) and ranks. Look things up in this order:
1. `scripts/ref_lookup.py "<terms>"`, which returns the matching sections of this digest.
2. The `tq-researcher` subagent (in Antigravity, `invoke_subagent` with `tq-researcher-prompt.md`), for questions the digest can't settle.

Page numbers are the printed page numbers. Command guidance and the user's rules outrank the T&Q (`writing-rules.md` §1).

## Ch 8: Step 6 (Edit) (pp. 91-102)
- **Active voice.** Put the doer first. Passive voice hides or delays the doer and makes the line longer (p. 96).
- **Unsmother verbs.** Use one specific verb, not a general verb plus a noun: "applies to", not "is applicable to"; "uses", not "makes use of" (p. 96).
- **Misused words.** Spell check misses homonyms (their/there, accept/except), so check them by eye (p. 96).
- **Parallelism.** Items in a list or series take the same grammatical form. If one item starts with a verb, they all do (p. 96).
- **Redundancy.** Cut modifiers and near-synonym pairs that add nothing: "standards and criteria" becomes "standards" (p. 97; also p. 102).
- **Misplaced modifiers.** Put a modifier next to the word it modifies. A dangling "-ing" phrase has to describe the doer (p. 97). An ambiguous adverb can read with either neighbor: "occasionally drive", not "drive to work occasionally" (pp. 97-98).
- **Subject-verb agreement** (pp. 98-99):
  - A phrase between the subject and the verb doesn't change the number: "team consisting of 36 people **is**".
  - "And" joins subjects into a plural. With "or" or "nor", the verb agrees with the nearer subject.
  - A collective noun treated as a unit takes a singular verb.
  - Each, every, everyone and anyone are singular.
- **Pronoun agreement.** Match the antecedent's number: "committee ... **its** report", not "their" (pp. 99-100).
- **Parenthetical commas.** An expression that can be removed without changing the meaning is set off by commas, with **both** commas present. A restrictive expression takes no commas (pp. 100-101).
- **Error list** (pp. 101-102): no clichés, hyperbole, double negatives, "irregardless", multiple exclamation points (!!!), or needless words. "It's" means only "it is"; the possessive is "its". The list jokes "Eschew ampersands & abbreviations" but concedes that EPRs and OPRs use them.

## Ch 19: Writing Better Bullet Statements (pp. 247-256)
- **Authority.** The T&Q gives general guidance. Follow the governing instructions and command guidance for each form (p. 247). Check local guidance on acronyms and abbreviations (p. 252).
- **Tone.** Positive words leave a lasting impression. Neutral or negative wording makes the person read as average or below (p. 248).
- **Step 1: Extract the facts** (pp. 248-249):
  - Isolate the specific action and tag it with a power verb.
  - Measure it: count, dollars, man-hours, people served.
  - Connect it to the bigger mission.
  - Ask the member and coworkers, and check source documents.
  - For each fact, ask whether it is solidly tied to **this one** accomplishment.
- **Step 2: Build the structure.** Sort the facts into the accomplishment (the what) and the impact (who, when, how, why) (p. 249).
  - **Accomplishment element.** Open with an action verb. An "-ly" adverb may intensify it, e.g. "Tenaciously processed ..." (pp. 249-251). The p. 250 verb list includes weak or odd entries (Attend, Helped, Utilized, Agitated); pick strong, precise verbs.
  - **One accomplishment per bullet.** Combining actions makes each one share, and dilute, the bullet's strength (p. 251).
  - **Impact element.** Show the effect and the level it reached: work center, unit, wing, AF, DoD. **Keep the impact scope consistent with the accomplishment.** A base-exercise records task doesn't "save the AF millions" (p. 251).
  - **Connectors** (p. 252):
    - an "-ing" word ("... Mobility Exercise ensuring all wing personnel met ...");
    - a semicolon ("...; all wing personnel met ...");
    - multiple impacts as `--` sub-bullets that start with verbs. Many commands forbid these, and they cost space.
    - The one-line 707 has no sub-bullets. The OPR convention of `--` before the impact comes from command guidance, not the T&Q.
- **Step 3: Streamline with ABS: accurate, brief, specific** (pp. 252-253).
  - *Accurate:* no stretching, exaggerating or embellishing, however minor. These are permanent official records (p. 252).
  - *Brief:* use the shortest clear words and cut or sharply reduce the following (p. 253):
    - articles (a, an, the);
    - helping verbs (can, could, may, might, must, ought, shall, should, will, would);
    - forms of be, have and do;
    - linking verbs of the senses (look, sound, smell, feel, taste);
    - the person's name when it is printed elsewhere on the form;
    - personal pronouns;
    - prepositions (over, under, in, during, within).
  - *Specific:* don't estimate or generalize. **No ranges ("10-20 units") and no rounded or hedged numbers ("approximately $1000", "nearly 3500")**; get the exact figure (p. 253).
- **Polishing** (pp. 253-255):
  - Verify facts by asking questions.
  - ABS doesn't have to run in A-B-S order.
  - Edit for shorter words and fewer words at the same time: "seriously corroded, broken, or missing" became "damaged"; "within 3 days by using" became "in 3 days using".
  - The final example uses "$3.4K" and drops "estimated". It also mixes "six" and "4 weeks"; don't copy that, keep numbers consistent (Ch 28).
- **Mechanics** (p. 256):
  - Main bullets start with `- `; sub-bullets use `--`, then `---`.
  - Use internal punctuation as needed. **Never use ending punctuation.**
  - **Always start with an action** (a verb or a modified verb). **Never start with a proper noun or pronoun.** Strat lines are the accepted OPR exception.
  - Minimize the ratee's name and avoid personal pronouns (he, she, his, her).

## Ch 25: Punctuation (pp. 295-330)
- **Open punctuation is the Air Force trend.** Use only what prevents misreading (p. 296). Evaluations, awards and other space-constrained forms typically use open punctuation, with **no serial comma**: "train, car or plane". Add the serial comma only when long items would be unclear (p. 297; also p. 304). Pick one style and stay consistent.
- If the punctuation gets confusing, restructure the line instead (p. 296).

**Apostrophe**
- Possessives take 's (officer's, ROTC's, business's). A plural ending in s takes only the apostrophe (Officers' Club). For joint possession, mark only the last noun (p. 298).
- **Plurals of abbreviations and capital letters take s alone: OPRs, EPRs, TRs, B-52s, 1960s** (p. 299). Use 's only for lowercase letters or where confusion would result (i's, A's, I's, U's). Write STOs, not STO's.
- Contractions mark omitted letters (p. 299). Avoid them in formal documents (p. 333).

**Comma**
- Don't put a comma before the second verb of a compound predicate that shares one subject (p. 304).
- Separate parallel adjectives with a comma (long, hot summer) when "and" could sit between them. Use no comma when the first adjective modifies the combination (heavy winter overcoat) (p. 304).
- Set off nonessential phrases with a pair of commas, and essential ones with none (p. 305).
- "etc." is discouraged. Never use it with "e.g.", "for example" or "such as" (p. 304).
- Add commas to prevent misreading, especially when two numbers would touch: "For each group of 20, 10 were rejected" (pp. 307-308).

**Dashes**
- The em dash (—) marks an abrupt break, gives emphasis to a second clause, or restates a thought (p. 309).
- **Dashes emphasize; parentheses de-emphasize** (pp. 309, 322).
- Leave no space before or after an em or en dash (p. 310).
- Typewriter forms are `---` for the em dash and `--` for the en dash (p. 310). The OPR habit of typing `--` as the impact dash comes from command convention.
- The en dash (–) joins inclusive ranges when there is no "from" or "between": "pp. 15–30", "0800–0900", but "from 3 to 10" (p. 310).

**Exclamation point**
- Use it for strong emotion (p. 314). On evaluation reports, use it **sparingly** so it keeps its impact (p. 314). Never stack them (p. 102).

**Hyphen**
- **Unit modifier before a noun: "4-hour sortie", "10-minute delay"** (p. 315). If the unit of measure is abbreviated, the T&Q drops the hyphen: "4 hr sortie" (pp. 315, 334, 356). **Command guidance outranks this:** SpOC 1.5.3.11 hyphenates compounds such as "32-mbr" and "5-wk", so keep those.
- Hyphenate a compound modifier **before** the noun but not after it: "up-to-date report" but "report is up to date"; "decision-making process" (pp. 316, 319).
- **Never hyphenate after an "-ly" adverb:** "highly skilled team" (p. 316).
- "and"/"or" compounds take hyphens: "cause-and-effect", "go/no-go decision" (p. 316).
- Suspended hyphen: "2-, 3- and 7-day", "long- and short-term" (p. 315).
- Letter-number designators: F-16, KC-10, B-1B (p. 315).
- **Prefixes are usually closed:** co, counter, inter, multi, non, over, post, pre, re, semi, sub, super, under (p. 317). Examples: "multimillion", "nonstandard", "preeminent".
- Hyphenate a prefix before a capital or a number (mid-June, pre-1914, non-European) and to avoid a doubled vowel (anti-inflammatory, multi-industry) (pp. 316, 318).
- **All-, self-, ex- and quasi- always take a hyphen** (pp. 317-319).
- High-, low-, well- and similar compounds are hyphenated before a noun and open after it (p. 317).
- "-wide" is usually closed (worldwide); hyphenate it only when the word gets cumbersome (p. 319).
- "-fold" takes a hyphen with numerals: "25-fold" (p. 318).
- Hyphenate to avoid a homonym: re-sign vs resign, re-cover vs recover (p. 316).

**Parentheses**
- Enclose independent explanatory material (p. 322). Commas, semicolons and dashes go **outside** the closing parenthesis (p. 322).

**Semicolon**
- Joins closely related independent clauses that have no conjunction (p. 329). In a bullet, it links the action to the result.
- Separates series items that contain commas (p. 330). No space before it, one space after it (p. 330).

**Other marks**
- **Colon:** no colon after a verb or preposition (p. 303). Military time takes no colon (1430) (p. 302). Ratios take a colon (5:1) (p. 302).
- **Period:** most abbreviations carry none (check local guidance) (p. 324).
- **Slash and ampersand:** Ch 25 has no rules for them; command guidance (CFC Att 2) governs.

## Ch 26: Abbreviations (pp. 331-340)
- Evaluation forms leave less room, so conveying more in less space matters (p. 331). **Use the shortest form appropriate to the document that clearly conveys the meaning** (pp. 332, 334).
- **Types** (pp. 332-333): an **acronym** is pronounced as a word (NATO); an **initialism** is pronounced letter by letter (TDY, PCS, DoD); a **contraction** uses an apostrophe (can't). Forms usually have a separate place for spelling out terms (Sec X on the 707).
- **Choose a or an by sound:** "an NCO", "an MSgt", but "a TDY" (p. 333).
- **Only approved abbreviations may be used on performance evaluations and award forms** (p. 334). Follow the organization's list (p. 335).
- Use abbreviations **sparingly, correctly and consistently** (p. 334).
- Avoid starting a sentence with an abbreviation, acronym or brevity code (p. 334). In OPR bullets the action verb comes first anyway.
- "United States" is the noun and "US" is the adjective: "US policy" (p. 334).
- **Units of measure:** a figure with the abbreviated unit: 3 mi, 55 mph, 50 lb (p. 334).
- **Ordinals:** 1st, **2d, 3d**, 4th (p. 334).
- **When both an abbreviation and a contraction exist, use the shorter:** "gov", not "gov't" (p. 334).
- Write abbreviations without periods unless confusion would result (in., no.) (p. 334).
- Abbreviate single words in lowercase: hosp, ltr (p. 334).
- **Plurals** (p. 335): where ambiguity could result, add a lowercase s (ltrs, TRs, AOs). **Never form a plural with an apostrophe.**
- **Don't capitalize the spelled-out words just because the acronym is capitalized:** OJT is "on the job training", OPSEC is "operations security"; but JCS is "Joint Chiefs of Staff", a proper noun (p. 335).
- **"Bottom Line: If in doubt, spell it out"** (p. 335).
- **Dates** (pp. 335-336): months Jan, Feb, Mar, Apr, May, Jun, Jul, Aug, Sep, Oct, Nov, Dec; fiscal and academic years FY14 and AY14.
- **Numbered organizations:** an abbreviated numbered unit drops the ordinal: "24th Air Force (24 AF)" (p. 338).
- **Latin:** e.g. means "for example" and i.e. means "that is". Never pair e.g. with etc. (pp. 339-340).

## Ch 27: Capitalization (pp. 341-354)
- Use the organization's style. **Be consistent throughout** (p. 341).
- **Proper vs common nouns:** capitalize the full name and lowercase the generic stand-in: Air War College, the college; 42d Air Base Wing, the wing (pp. 344, 351).
- **Exercises, operations and military concepts are capitalized:** Exercise ULCHI FOCUS LENS, Operation ENDURING FREEDOM (p. 344). The nickname is written in all caps.
- **Government and military bodies** (pp. 345-346): United States Air Force, Air Force; Department of Defense (DoD); "armed forces" is lowercase; full titles of directorates are capitalized, "the directorate" is not.
- **Military rank** (p. 348): capitalize a rank used with a name; lowercase it when it stands alone ("the colonel", "30 majors"). **Never mix abbreviated and full forms: "Lt Col", not "Lt Colonel".**
- **Medals and awards:** capitalize specific names (Legion of Merit) (p. 348).
- **Titles** (pp. 348-350): **lowercase job titles that stand alone:** "the commander", "the squadron commander", "director of research".
- **Numbered units** (pp. 351, 358): written in full, a unit keeps its ordinal ("42d Air Base Wing"); **abbreviated, it drops it: "42 ABW", "79 FS".**
- **Degrees and courses** (p. 351): "master's degree", but Master of Science, MS, PhD.
- **Regions:** capitalize a specific region (Far East, Central Europe) and lowercase a general direction (p. 352).

## Ch 28: Numbers (pp. 355-362)
- **Figures emphasize; words de-emphasize** (p. 355).
- **The organization's preferred style overrides the general rules** (p. 355). The general rule is **words for one through nine and figures for 10 and up**, with commas from 1,000 (pp. 355, 359). Money, percents, units of measure, multipliers, ratios and designators stay as figures. How strictly an OPR follows it is unit policy (`settings.policies.small_numbers`, see `writing-rules.md` §1).
- **Related series:** if any number in the series is 10 or more, all of them are figures (p. 358).
- **Money** (p. 356): omit ".00" unless other amounts in the line have cents; use commas in 4-digit and larger numbers (5,280). "$300,000", **not "$300 thousand"** (p. 360). K, M and B follow command guidance (the T&Q's own bullet example uses "$3.4K", p. 255).
- **Percent:** "6 percent" in text (p. 357); OPRs use % by command convention.
- **Ranges** (pp. 310, 356): "from X to Y" in words, or an en dash with no "from"; ABS discourages ranges in bullets anyway (p. 253).
- **Time:** military time with no "hours" (by 0800) (p. 356).
- **Dates:** "5 Jun 04"; if the month is abbreviated, abbreviate the year too (p. 357). FY04, AY03.
- **Adjacent numbers:** spell out the first when two numbers modify in sequence: "two 4-hour sorties" (p. 360).
- **Plurals of figures add s alone:** 1990s, F-16s (p. 359).
- **Rounded and indefinite numbers** ("hundreds of customers") are spelled out in prose (p. 360); ABS says to replace them with exact figures (p. 253).

## Ranks
From Ch 15, Military Ranks and Abbreviations (pp. 211-212), plus p. 348.
- **Air Force (and Space Force officer) forms:** 2d Lt, 1st Lt, Capt, Maj, Lt Col, Col, Brig Gen, Maj Gen, Lt Gen, Gen (p. 212).
- **Contrasting styles** (p. 212): Army all caps (2LT, CPT, MAJ, LTC); Marine Corps 2nd Lt and LtCol; Navy all caps (ENS, LCDR, CDR). These forms are **wrong** in a DAF OPR.
- Never mix abbreviated and full forms ("Lt Col", not "Lt Colonel"). Capitalize a rank only when it is used with a name (p. 348).
- For USSF enlisted ranks, see `data/ranks.json`, which follows the spaceforce.mil rank-names article.
